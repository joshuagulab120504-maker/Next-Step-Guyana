# Next Step Guyana — Mentor Journey Interview Framework

The purpose of each interview is to capture decisions, uncertainty, trade-offs and turning points that Navi can retrieve safely and accurately. It should not produce generic biography copy or imply that one mentor’s route is the correct formula for a student.

## 1. Starting context

1. Where in Guyana did you grow up and what school stages did you complete?
2. At Forms 1–3, what did you think your future might look like?
3. Which interests felt natural, and which developed later?
4. What opportunities, limitations or responsibilities shaped your choices?
5. What did adults or peers expect you to do, and how did that affect you?

## 2. Academic decisions

1. Which CSEC subjects did you select, and why?
2. Which subjects did you write, what preparation mattered and what would you change?
3. Did you choose CAPE, Sixth Form, TVET, work, university or another route?
4. What alternatives did you seriously consider?
5. What information did you have at the time, and what information was missing?
6. Which requirements surprised you?
7. Did cost, location, transport, family or timing change the route you took?

## 3. Decision forks

For every major fork, ask:

1. What were the two or three options?
2. What were you afraid might happen?
3. What mattered most in the decision?
4. Who influenced you?
5. What evidence helped?
6. What trade-off did you accept?
7. What did you choose?
8. What happened afterwards?
9. Knowing what you know now, what would you tell a student at that same fork?

Important forks include CAPE versus direct tertiary entry, local versus overseas study, academic versus technical routes, work versus continued study, changing fields, accepting or rejecting a scholarship and balancing leadership with grades.

## 4. Experiences that tested the path

1. Which club, project, job, volunteer activity or conversation helped confirm an interest?
2. Which experience showed that a field was not what you expected?
3. What was the smallest useful experiment a student could try?
4. Which skills mattered before you had formal credentials?
5. What failure, rejection or delay changed your plan?

## 5. Applications and transitions

1. How did you find programmes, scholarships or work opportunities?
2. What did a strong application require?
3. Which deadlines or documents were easy to underestimate?
4. Who wrote recommendations, and how did you build those relationships?
5. What was the transition into the next institution or workplace actually like?
6. What support did you wish existed?

## 6. Identity, belonging and wellbeing

1. When did you feel uncertain or out of place?
2. How did being Guyanese shape the opportunities or challenges you faced?
3. What helped you recover from setbacks?
4. What boundaries should a student protect while pursuing ambitious goals?
5. What advice would you never give, even if it worked for you?

## 7. Current destination and future direction

1. What does your present work or study involve day to day?
2. Which parts are rewarding, difficult or misunderstood?
3. Which school subjects and experiences actually became useful?
4. What routes can lead into this field besides the route you took?
5. How is your own Northern Star still changing?

## 8. Structured metadata for matching

Record these fields separately from the narrative:

- School stage at each decision
- Region and school context, where consented
- CSEC and CAPE subjects
- Qualifications and institutions
- Career fields and industries
- Key decision forks
- Routes considered and route selected
- Constraints: cost, location, timing, family, transport, work
- Learning preference
- Interests and values
- Programmes, scholarships and experiences
- Mistakes, setbacks and recoveries
- Advice themes
- Mentor availability: story only, feed, pod or one-to-one
- Consent level for each anecdote
- Verification status and last review date

## 9. Safe use by Navi

- Separate verified facts from personal experience.
- Attribute anecdotes to the mentor only when consent allows it.
- Never present a mentor’s choice as the student’s required choice.
- Explain why a mentor story was matched.
- Offer contrasting journeys when reasonable.
- Never expose private student progress to mentors without explicit permissions.
- Escalate safeguarding, mental-health, legal, financial or high-stakes questions to approved human processes.
- Allow mentors to review, correct or withdraw their stories.
